Itihasa — the great epics

The Ramayana and the Mahabharata are itihasa, "thus it was" — traditional narrative sacred to Hindus, transmitted and dated quite differently from the archaeological record. They live apart from the Bharat Chronicle timeline because their chronology isn't settled the way the Indus Valley's stratigraphy is.

Exiled to the forest for fourteen years by a promise Dasharatha owed Kaikeyi, Rama loses Sita to Ravana, recovers her with an army of vanaras led by Hanuman, and returns to be crowned in Ayodhya — the return itself still marked every year as Diwali.

A rigged dice game strips the Pandavas of their kingdom and sends them into thirteen years' exile; Duryodhana's refusal to return so much as "land enough for a needle's point" on their return leaves war the only recourse.

RadhaKrishna's beloved companion during his youth in Vrindavan, central to later bhakti devotional tradition — though she is not named in the Mahabharata itself.

Krishna kills his tyrant uncle Kansa, later relocates the Yadavas to Dwarka after Jarasandha's sieges, and elopes with Rukmini before her forced wedding to a rival king. As Arjuna's charioteer at Kurukshetra, his battlefield counsel is recorded as the Bhagavad Gita.

The Battle of Kurukshetra

Cause
Succession dispute between cousins — Duryodhana's refusal to restore the Pandavas' kingdom after their exile.
Location
The plain of Kurukshetra, in present-day Haryana.
Duration
Eighteen days, fought in successive named formations (vyuha) under a rotating cast of Kaurava commanders.
Turning point
Arjuna's crisis of conscience before the battle, resolved by Krishna's counsel — recorded as the Bhagavad Gita.
Outcome
The Pandavas prevail; Duryodhana and nearly the entire Kaurava line are killed. Yudhishthira is crowned king of Hastinapura.
Aftermath
Traditionally reckoned as the close of Dvapara Yuga and the start of Kali Yuga, the present age.

Itihasa timeline

Each epic's story in the order it is told, book by book. Neither has calendar dates: tradition places the Ramayana in the Treta Yuga and the Mahabharata at the close of the Dvapara Yuga, and the war at Kurukshetra is the hinge into the present age.

Purana — the Hindu pantheon

A separate category from the epics above: the Ramayana and Mahabharata are itihasa, narratives about kings and wars fought by (and among) the gods' avatars and descendants. What follows is Puranic cosmology itself — the gods' own lineages, kept here on their own rather than folded into either epic's family tree.

Kali, Durga and Parvati are traditionally understood as forms of one Goddess (Devi / Shakti) rather than separate individuals: Parvati as Shiva's gentle consort, Durga and the fiercer Kali as her warrior aspects, invoked against demons such as Mahishasura. The three gods above are usually read together as one cosmic function — Brahma creates, Vishnu preserves (descending as an avatar whenever dharma is threatened), Shiva destroys to allow renewal. Consort traditions for Ganesha and Kartikeya vary significantly by region and sect; both are shown here rather than picked as the single "correct" version.
